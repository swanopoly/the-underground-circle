# Cancer Clinical Trials And Care Navigation

Date: 2026-06-08

Status: Dated research report. Educational only. Clinical trial and care decisions should be reviewed with licensed clinicians and the trial team.

## Purpose

Cancer research moves quickly, but users need a simple way to decide what evidence is credible and what question to ask next. This guide turns the wiki into a care-navigation research aid without letting it become a medical decision engine.

## Records checklist

Before comparing treatment information or searching for trials, collect:

- exact cancer diagnosis, site, histology, and pathology report
- stage, grade, risk group, and date of staging
- biomarker and molecular testing results
- scans and scan reports
- treatment history with start dates, stop dates, response, and side effects
- surgery and radiation summaries
- current medications, allergies, major health conditions, and performance status if known
- family history and genetic testing if relevant
- care goals and practical constraints such as travel, work, caregiving, cost, and support

## Clinical trial basics

| Trial concept | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Phase 1 | Tests safety, dose, and early signals. May include many cancer types or biomarker-defined groups. |
| Phase 2 | Tests activity in a more defined group. Often looks at response, progression, or disease control. |
| Phase 3 | Compares a new approach against a standard approach in a larger group. Often supports guideline or approval changes. |
| Phase 4 | Studies treatments after approval in broader real-world or long-term contexts. |
| Eligibility criteria | Rules for who can join, including cancer type, stage, biomarkers, prior treatments, organ function, age, and health status. |
| Endpoint | What the study measures: safety, response, survival, symptoms, quality of life, or another defined outcome. |
| Randomization | Assigns participants to groups by study design rather than choice. Not every trial is randomized. |
| Standard of care | The usual evidence-supported approach outside the trial, which should be part of the comparison conversation. |

## Trial matching questions

Ask these before treating a trial listing as relevant:

- Is the trial open and recruiting?
- Does it match the exact cancer type, stage, and biomarkers?
- Does it allow the user's prior treatments?
- Is the trial local, remote, or travel-heavy?
- What visits, biopsies, scans, and labs are required?
- What costs are routine care versus research-covered?
- What is the alternative if the user does not enroll?
- What are the likely benefits, uncertainties, and harms?
- Who is the sponsor, and what independent oversight exists?

## Credible source hierarchy

| Stronger sources | Use |
| --- | --- |
| Treating oncology team and tumor board | Personalizes evidence to the user and records. |
| National guidelines and major cancer centers | Good for current standard pathways when accessible. |
| NCI, CDC, NIH, WHO, USPSTF, FDA | Strong for public health, definitions, prevention, trials, and regulatory context. |
| Peer-reviewed studies | Useful when the population and endpoint match the question. |
| Trial registries | Good for eligibility and status, but not proof that a treatment works. |
| Patient advocacy organizations | Useful for support and education; verify medical claims against primary or guideline sources. |
| News, blogs, social media, clinic marketing | Use only as leads to better sources. Do not treat as proof. |

## Misinformation guardrails

Flag and escalate claims that:

- advertise one cure for all cancers
- claim chemotherapy, radiation, surgery, or immunotherapy never works
- require buying a supplement, device, secret protocol, or private membership
- rely only on testimonials
- tell users to delay urgent care or abandon prescribed therapy
- claim that oxygen, alkaline diets, detoxes, coffee enemas, or sugar elimination alone cure cancer
- use "natural" as a substitute for evidence, dose, safety, and monitoring
- cite cell or animal studies as direct proof of human cure

## How the app should help

The chat/wiki should make cancer research calmer and safer:

- summarize official sources in plain language
- build a question list for the clinician visit
- organize records and missing data
- explain what a pathology report, biomarker, or trial listing means
- compare source quality
- identify when a prompt is asking for medical advice and switch to an educational boundary
- encourage urgent care for emergency symptoms

It should not:

- choose a treatment
- tell the user to start, stop, or change medicine
- replace an oncologist
- promise outcomes
- pressure a user into or away from a trial
