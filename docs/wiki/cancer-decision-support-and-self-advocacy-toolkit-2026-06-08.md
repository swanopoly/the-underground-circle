# Cancer Decision Support And Self-Advocacy Toolkit

Date: 2026-06-08

Status: Dated research report. Educational only. This guide helps users organize questions and information for shared decision-making. It does not diagnose, prescribe, or replace a licensed oncology team.

## Purpose

The best cancer decision is not always the most aggressive option, the newest option, the cheapest option, or the option that sounds most hopeful online. The best decision is the one that matches the evidence, the exact cancer situation, the user's values, practical constraints, and clinician review.

This guide gives users a structured way to prepare for decisions about diagnosis, treatment, second opinions, clinical trials, supportive care, surveillance, or end-of-life choices.

## The decision frame

| Decision layer | What to ask | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Medical facts | What exactly is the cancer type, stage, grade, subtype, biomarker profile, and treatment line? | A treatment that fits one subtype or stage may be wrong for another. |
| Options | What are all reasonable options, including standard care, clinical trials, monitoring, symptom-focused care, and no immediate treatment when appropriate? | Users cannot make an informed choice if only one path is visible. |
| Benefits | What is the expected benefit, and how is it measured? | Response, remission, cure, survival, symptom relief, function, and quality of life are different outcomes. |
| Harms | What side effects, complications, late effects, and practical burdens are likely? | Toxicity, recovery time, work disruption, fertility, function, cognition, appearance, and independence can matter as much as disease control. |
| Timing | How urgent is the decision, and what information is still pending? | Some cancers need fast treatment. Others allow time for biomarkers, second opinions, or fertility planning. |
| Personal priorities | What outcome matters most to the user right now? | The best choice can change depending on goals, values, family responsibilities, work, travel, faith, finances, and tolerance for uncertainty. |
| Logistics | Where will treatment happen, how often, for how long, and who helps? | Transportation, lodging, caregiving, time off work, and insurance can make or break a plan. |
| Proof and follow-up | How will the team know if the plan is working, and what happens if it is not? | Users need a monitoring plan, response criteria, side-effect plan, and next-step trigger. |

## Shared decision-making

Shared decision-making means the care team and patient make decisions together using medical evidence and the person's values, goals, concerns, available options, risks, and benefits. A useful structure is:

1. Seek participation.
2. Help compare options.
3. Assess values and preferences.
4. Reach a decision together.
5. Evaluate the decision and revisit it as needed.

For the app, the important pattern is: the chat should help users prepare for that conversation, not make the decision for them.

## Questions before treatment starts

Ask the oncology team:

- What type and stage of cancer do I have?
- What information is still missing?
- Do we need more pathology review, imaging, biomarker testing, genetic counseling, or specialist review?
- What are the reasonable options for my cancer type and stage?
- What do you recommend, and why is that the best fit for me?
- How urgent is this decision?
- What happens if I wait for a second opinion or test result?
- What is the chance this treatment helps, and what does "helps" mean here?
- What are the most common side effects?
- What side effects require calling the team immediately?
- How will this affect work, fertility, sex, cognition, mobility, appearance, eating, sleep, pain, or independence?
- Is a clinical trial reasonable for me?
- Who is my main contact after hours or on weekends?
- What should I do if I cannot afford or access part of the plan?

## Second opinion checklist

A second opinion can be especially valuable when:

- the cancer is rare, aggressive, advanced, recurrent, or hard to classify
- major surgery, radiation, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, targeted therapy, transplant, or fertility-impacting treatment is being considered
- pathology is unusual or uncertain
- biomarker testing could change treatment
- the plan has high toxicity or irreversible consequences
- the user feels rushed, confused, unheard, or missing key information
- there are several reasonable options
- a clinical trial may be relevant

Before the second opinion, gather:

- pathology reports and, if requested, pathology slides or blocks
- imaging reports and image disks or portal access
- lab reports and tumor marker trends
- molecular, biomarker, and genetic test reports
- treatment summary and dates
- medication list, allergies, and major health conditions
- insurance information and referral requirements
- the exact question you want answered

## How to interpret statistics without getting misled

| Statistic or phrase | What to ask |
| --- | --- |
| Response rate | Did tumors shrink, and for how long? Did people live longer or feel better? |
| Progression-free survival | How long until the cancer grew or worsened? Did overall survival or quality of life improve? |
| Overall survival | Was there a clear survival difference, and in what population? |
| Relative risk reduction | What is the absolute benefit? A large relative number can be a small absolute difference. |
| Median survival | Half did better and half did worse. What predicts which side a person may fall on? |
| Cure rate | What stage and follow-up period does this apply to? |
| "Promising" | Was this a cell study, animal study, phase 1 trial, randomized trial, guideline, or marketing claim? |
| "Natural" | What is the evidence, dose, contamination risk, interaction risk, and monitoring plan? |

## Decision red flags

Pause and ask for more help if:

- the team cannot explain the goal of treatment
- the user does not know what cancer type or stage they have
- irreversible treatment is planned before key pathology, staging, or biomarker information is available, unless the team explains why waiting is unsafe
- the user is told not to seek a second opinion
- costs, transportation, caregiver needs, or work impacts are ignored
- side effects are minimized or not explained
- an online source promises a cure for all cancers
- a seller pressures the user to buy a supplement, device, or secret protocol
- someone tells the user to stop prescribed care without clinician oversight

## App behavior standard

When a user asks for help deciding, the chat should:

- summarize the choice without choosing for the user
- list missing information
- build a question list for the oncology team
- ask what matters most to the user
- compare benefits, harms, timing, logistics, and uncertainty
- suggest second opinion questions when appropriate
- recommend urgent clinician contact for emergency symptoms
- cite official sources and keep the medical boundary visible

The chat should not:

- rank treatments as "best" without patient-specific clinical review
- tell a user to start, stop, delay, or change treatment
- overstate early research
- treat cost, caregiving, fertility, pain, or quality of life as secondary
