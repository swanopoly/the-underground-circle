# Cancer Screening, Prevention, And Risk Guide

Date: 2026-06-08

Status: Dated research report. This is educational research support only. Screening and risk decisions depend on age, anatomy, pregnancy status, prior results, family history, genetic risk, exposures, symptoms, immune status, and clinician review.

## Purpose

Cancer prevention and screening are different tasks:

- Prevention lowers risk before cancer develops.
- Screening looks for cancer or precancer before symptoms.
- Diagnostic testing investigates symptoms or abnormal screening results.

The wiki should keep those lanes separate so users do not confuse a general screening rule with a personalized medical plan.

## High-confidence prevention levers

| Lever | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Avoid tobacco and secondhand smoke | Tobacco is tied to many cancers, especially lung cancer, and also cancers of the mouth, throat, bladder, pancreas, kidney, cervix, stomach, liver, colon, rectum, and blood. |
| HPV vaccination | HPV vaccination helps prevent HPV-related cancers, including cervical and other anogenital and oropharyngeal cancers. |
| Hepatitis B vaccination and hepatitis care | Hepatitis B and C can raise liver cancer risk; vaccination and treatment reduce preventable harm. |
| UV protection | UV exposure raises skin cancer risk, including melanoma and nonmelanoma skin cancers. |
| Limit alcohol | Alcohol is a cancer risk factor, including for cancers of the mouth, throat, esophagus, liver, breast, colon, and rectum. |
| Healthy weight, physical activity, and nutrition | Obesity and inactivity are linked with multiple cancers. These are population-level risk levers, not guarantees for an individual. |
| Occupational and environmental controls | Reduce exposure to asbestos, radon, certain chemicals, ionizing radiation, and workplace carcinogens when applicable. |
| Infection prevention and treatment | HPV, hepatitis B/C, H. pylori, EBV, HIV-related immune suppression, and some parasites are relevant in different cancer types. |
| Genetic risk assessment when indicated | Strong family history, early-onset cancers, or multiple related cancers can justify genetic counseling or testing. |

## Screening map

This table summarizes major U.S. population screening lanes. Always recheck the current USPSTF, CDC, ACS, and specialty guidance before using exact ages or intervals.

| Cancer | Common screening method | General research note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Breast | Mammography | USPSTF recommends biennial screening mammography for women ages 40 to 74. Higher-risk people may need earlier or different screening. |
| Cervical | Cervical cytology and/or high-risk HPV testing | Screening depends on age and prior results. HPV vaccination does not remove the need for age-appropriate screening. |
| Colorectal | Colonoscopy, stool tests, CT colonography, flexible sigmoidoscopy, or other accepted strategies | USPSTF recommends screening adults ages 45 to 75. Method and interval vary by test and risk. |
| Lung | Low-dose CT | USPSTF recommends annual low-dose CT for adults ages 50 to 80 with a 20 pack-year smoking history who currently smoke or quit within the past 15 years. |
| Prostate | PSA-based screening discussion | USPSTF frames PSA screening for ages 55 to 69 as an individual decision and recommends against routine PSA screening at age 70 or older. |
| Skin | Skin exam when risk, symptoms, or clinician concern exists | Population-wide screening evidence is different from checking a changing lesion. Concerning lesions should be evaluated. |
| Liver | Ultrasound and/or blood tests in high-risk groups | Not a general population screen. Used in selected high-risk groups such as some people with cirrhosis or chronic hepatitis. |
| Ovarian, pancreatic, thyroid, testicular, head and neck | Usually no broad population screening for average-risk asymptomatic adults | High-risk families, genetic syndromes, symptoms, or incidental findings change the workup. Avoid turning low-value screening into anxiety or harm. |

## Symptoms that should not be reduced to a chatbot answer

Symptoms are not proof of cancer, but persistent or concerning changes deserve medical attention. Examples include:

- unexplained weight loss, fever, night sweats, or fatigue
- new lump, swelling, or persistent enlarged lymph node
- blood in stool, urine, sputum, or unexpected vaginal bleeding
- persistent cough, hoarseness, difficulty swallowing, or shortness of breath
- changing mole, non-healing sore, or unusual skin lesion
- persistent abdominal pain, bloating, change in bowel habits, or jaundice
- new neurologic symptoms such as seizure, weakness, severe headache, or vision changes
- persistent bone pain or unexplained fracture

The safe product behavior is to suggest clinical evaluation rather than interpret symptoms as a diagnosis.

## How agents should present prevention content

Agents should:

- state that screening guidance changes and depends on personal risk
- link to official sources
- avoid giving a diagnosis
- encourage users to discuss age, risk, family history, symptoms, and prior results with a clinician
- distinguish evidence-backed prevention from supplements, detox claims, or single-cause theories
- keep urgent symptoms visible instead of burying them in long educational text

Agents should not:

- tell a user they do or do not have cancer
- tell a user to skip a biopsy, scan, prescribed therapy, or clinician visit
- recommend unproven cancer cures
- imply screening is always good for every cancer
- pretend a general guideline replaces a clinician who knows the user
