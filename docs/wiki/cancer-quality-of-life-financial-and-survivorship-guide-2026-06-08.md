# Cancer Quality Of Life, Financial, And Survivorship Guide

Date: 2026-06-08

Status: Dated research report. Educational only. Users should discuss symptoms, side effects, finances, and survivorship needs with their oncology team, primary care team, social worker, palliative care team, or other qualified professionals.

## Purpose

Cancer decisions are not only about shrinking tumors. People also need to understand side effects, pain, function, mental health, family roles, costs, work, fertility, sexuality, survivorship, and caregiver support. These practical issues can change what decision feels right.

This guide helps the wiki and app keep the whole person visible.

## Quality-of-life decision map

| Area | What to ask |
| --- | --- |
| Pain and symptoms | What symptoms should improve with treatment, what may worsen, and who manages them? |
| Fatigue and sleep | How common is fatigue, how long can it last, and what support exists? |
| Eating and weight | Will treatment affect appetite, taste, swallowing, nausea, bowel habits, or nutrition? |
| Mobility and function | Will treatment affect walking, lifting, driving, work, exercise, or daily tasks? |
| Cognition and mood | Could treatment affect memory, attention, anxiety, depression, or sleep? |
| Nerve, heart, lung, kidney, liver, or endocrine effects | Which organs need monitoring before, during, and after treatment? |
| Fertility, sex, and intimacy | Is fertility preservation, contraception, menopause support, sexual health care, or pelvic health support needed? |
| Appearance and identity | Will hair, skin, scars, voice, swallowing, ostomy, breast shape, or body image change? |
| Infection risk | When should the user call for fever, chills, wound changes, cough, urinary symptoms, or other infection signs? |
| Caregiver needs | Who helps with visits, medications, meals, transportation, children, pets, bills, and emergencies? |
| Work and school | What accommodations, leave, disability paperwork, or schedule changes may be needed? |
| Costs | What is covered, what might be out of pocket, and who can help navigate bills? |

## Palliative care is not the same as giving up

Palliative care focuses on preventing or treating symptoms, side effects, emotional distress, social needs, spiritual concerns, caregiver strain, and practical problems. It can be used at any stage of cancer and can happen alongside cancer-directed treatment.

Users should consider asking about palliative care when there is:

- pain, nausea, fatigue, appetite loss, shortness of breath, insomnia, neuropathy, or distress
- advanced, metastatic, recurrent, or high-symptom disease
- difficult treatment tradeoffs
- caregiver overload
- uncertainty about goals of care
- frequent hospital or emergency visits
- need for help communicating among multiple teams

## Cost and financial toxicity

Financial toxicity means harm related to the cost of medical care. It can include medical bills, drug costs, travel, lodging, childcare, reduced income, debt, insurance problems, and skipped medication because of cost.

Questions to ask early:

- Is this doctor, hospital, imaging center, lab, pharmacy, and treatment in network?
- What prior authorization is needed?
- What are the estimated out-of-pocket costs?
- Are there lower-cost equivalent options?
- Are copay, drug manufacturer, foundation, charity, hospital, or social-work resources available?
- What costs are routine care versus research costs in a clinical trial?
- Who can help with appeals if insurance denies coverage?
- Can visits, labs, scans, or treatments be coordinated to reduce travel burden?
- What work, disability, leave, transportation, lodging, childcare, or caregiver resources exist?

## Practical records to keep

Keep a simple folder or digital record with:

- diagnosis, stage, grade, and subtype
- pathology reports
- surgery reports
- radiation summaries
- chemotherapy, immunotherapy, targeted therapy, hormone therapy, or transplant summaries
- scan reports and dates
- biomarker and genetic test reports
- medication list and allergies
- side-effect log
- emergency contact numbers
- insurance approvals and denial letters
- bills, explanation of benefits, payment plans, and assistance applications
- survivorship care plan after treatment

## Call-the-team planning

Every user should know:

- who to call during business hours
- who to call nights, weekends, and holidays
- when to call instead of using the portal
- when to go to urgent care or the emergency department
- what fever threshold matters during treatment
- what symptoms are expected versus urgent for their specific regimen
- what medications they should not take without asking

The app should not give universal emergency thresholds as if they apply to everyone. It should prompt the user to get the thresholds from their oncology team and surface urgent-care language for severe or rapidly worsening symptoms.

## Survivorship questions

After treatment, users may need a survivorship plan that covers:

- follow-up visit schedule
- scan and lab schedule
- signs of recurrence to report
- late effects to monitor
- primary care and specialist responsibilities
- vaccines and infection prevention when relevant
- heart, bone, fertility, endocrine, cognitive, dental, swallowing, lymphedema, neuropathy, or mental health follow-up when relevant
- return-to-work or school planning
- exercise, nutrition, sleep, and rehabilitation support
- copies of treatment records to keep

## Caregiver support

Caregivers may coordinate appointments, medications, transportation, meals, records, insurance, symptoms, emotional support, and daily activities. Their needs should be explicit, not invisible.

Caregiver questions:

- What tasks actually need help?
- Who can cover transportation, meals, children, pets, bills, or overnight support?
- What should caregivers watch for?
- What medical tasks are safe for caregivers to do, and what requires clinical help?
- Who has permission to receive medical information?
- What respite or counseling support exists?
- What is the backup plan if the main caregiver gets sick or overwhelmed?

## Advanced cancer and goals of care

When cancer is advanced, recurrent, or no longer responding, decisions can become deeply personal. Users may need to compare:

- more cancer-directed treatment
- a clinical trial
- symptom-focused care
- palliative care
- hospice when curative treatment is no longer the goal
- care at home versus hospital-based care
- time with family, travel, milestones, comfort, or specific life goals

The safest app behavior is to help users name their values and questions, then encourage a direct goals-of-care conversation with their clinicians and trusted people.

## App behavior standard

For quality-of-life and survivorship prompts, the chat should:

- ask what the user is trying to decide
- ask what symptoms, burdens, or practical limits matter most
- suggest questions for the oncology team, social worker, palliative care team, financial counselor, or survivorship clinic
- help organize records and logs
- flag urgent symptoms for clinician contact
- include caregiver and cost questions
- keep source-backed education separate from care instructions

It should not:

- minimize side effects
- treat cost or transportation as nonmedical
- imply palliative care means stopping all treatment
- provide medication dosing or symptom-management orders
- tell a user to ignore severe or worsening symptoms
