# Cancer Diagnosis, Staging, Biomarkers, And Treatment Guide

Date: 2026-06-08

Status: Dated research report. Educational only. Cancer diagnosis and treatment require qualified clinical care. This guide explains concepts so users can organize research and ask better questions.

## Purpose

The most common mistake in cancer research is skipping from a cancer name to a treatment idea. Good oncology research moves through diagnosis, stage, pathology, biomarkers, patient context, treatment goal, and evidence quality.

## The evidence loop

| Step | What it answers |
| --- | --- |
| Presentation | Was the cancer found by symptoms, screening, imaging, blood work, or incidental finding? |
| Diagnostic workup | What imaging, endoscopy, lab tests, or exams are needed to understand the problem? |
| Tissue diagnosis | What did biopsy, surgery, cytology, marrow exam, or pathology show? |
| Staging | Where is the cancer, how large is it, are nodes involved, and has it spread? |
| Grade and histology | How abnormal do the cells look, and what subtype is this? |
| Biomarkers | Are there tumor markers, hormone receptors, mutations, fusions, immune markers, or inherited risks that change care? |
| Treatment goal | Is the goal cure, control, symptom relief, prevention of recurrence, or surveillance? |
| Verification | What proof shows the task succeeded: pathology, imaging response, lab trend, symptom improvement, quality of life, or survival endpoint? |

## Staging and grading

Staging describes the extent of cancer. Many solid tumors use TNM concepts:

- T: size or local extent of the primary tumor
- N: whether nearby lymph nodes are involved
- M: whether distant metastasis is present

Some cancers use different systems. Blood cancers, brain tumors, pediatric cancers, and gynecologic cancers can have specialized staging or risk systems. Grade describes how abnormal or aggressive the cancer cells appear, but grade does not mean the same thing in every cancer.

## Biomarkers and genetics

Biomarkers can help diagnose, classify, select treatment, predict risk, or monitor disease. They do not all work the same way.

| Biomarker type | Examples | Research use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tumor markers in blood | PSA, CA-125, CA 19-9, CEA, AFP, beta-hCG, LDH, thyroglobulin | Sometimes useful for monitoring or selected diagnosis contexts, but many are not good screening tests for average-risk people. |
| Hormone receptors | ER, PR, androgen receptor | Can guide endocrine or hormone-directed therapy in selected cancers. |
| Growth factor receptors | HER2, EGFR, MET | Can guide targeted therapy in selected cancers. |
| Gene mutations and fusions | BRCA1/2, KRAS, NRAS, BRAF, ALK, ROS1, RET, NTRK, IDH, KIT, PDGFRA | Can change treatment options, inherited-risk counseling, or trial matching. |
| Immune and repair markers | MSI, mismatch repair, tumor mutational burden, PD-L1 | Can help identify immunotherapy relevance in selected settings. |
| Blood cancer markers | Flow cytometry, cytogenetics, BCR-ABL, FLT3, NPM1, JAK2, myeloma cytogenetics | Can define subtype, prognosis, measurable residual disease, and targeted treatment. |
| Germline genetics | BRCA1/2, Lynch syndrome genes, APC, TP53, PTEN, RET, VHL, MEN genes and others | Inherited findings can affect screening, family counseling, surgery decisions, and therapy in selected contexts. |

## Treatment families

| Treatment family | What it does | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Surgery | Removes cancer, precancer, nodes, or affected tissue. | Can be curative for some localized cancers. Margins, function, reconstruction, and recovery matter. |
| Radiation therapy | Uses targeted radiation to kill or control cancer cells. | Can be definitive, adjuvant, palliative, or combined with systemic therapy. |
| Chemotherapy | Uses drugs that affect rapidly dividing cells. | Can be curative in some cancers and palliative in others. Side effects and timing vary widely. |
| Immunotherapy | Uses the immune system to recognize or attack cancer. | Includes checkpoint inhibitors, cellular therapy, monoclonal antibodies, vaccines in selected contexts, and other approaches. |
| Targeted therapy | Blocks cancer-specific pathways, mutations, fusions, or receptors. | Requires the right cancer context and often biomarker testing. |
| Hormone therapy | Blocks or lowers hormone signaling. | Common in breast and prostate cancer and selected gynecologic contexts. |
| Stem cell or bone marrow transplant | Replaces or rescues marrow after intensive therapy or treats immune/blood cancers. | Used in selected blood cancers and high-risk conditions. |
| Interventional and local therapies | Ablation, embolization, liver-directed therapy, endoscopy, local injections. | Often used for selected organ-limited disease, palliation, or when surgery is not right. |
| Supportive and palliative care | Treats symptoms, side effects, function, distress, nutrition, pain, and goals of care. | Appropriate alongside active treatment, not only at end of life. |
| Surveillance and active monitoring | Watches low-risk disease with structured follow-up. | Used in selected cancers where immediate treatment may not improve outcomes. |
| Clinical trials | Tests prevention, screening, diagnostic, treatment, or supportive strategies. | Especially important for rare, advanced, recurrent, or biomarker-defined cancers. |

## Questions to ask before trusting a cancer article

- What exact cancer subtype and stage does this source discuss?
- Is the source about humans, animals, cells, a phase 1 trial, a randomized trial, or a guideline?
- Is the treatment approved, investigational, off-label, or theoretical?
- What was the comparison group?
- What outcomes improved: response rate, progression-free survival, overall survival, symptoms, toxicity, or quality of life?
- Does the source include harms, not just benefits?
- Does it apply to this age group, biomarker profile, prior treatments, and health context?
- Was the source written by a neutral institution, a guideline body, a journal, a clinic, a seller, or an influencer?

## Agent output standard for cancer tasks

When the chat summarizes cancer information, it should produce:

- the educational boundary
- the exact cancer and subtype if known
- stage or "stage unknown"
- biomarkers known, unknown, or not applicable
- source date and source type
- what is established, what is uncertain, and what requires clinician review
- next safe research step, not a medical order
